Names in Fashion

No company sells baby names, and no ad tells parents what to pick. Yet names rise and fall like clothing styles, which makes them one of the cleanest ways to watch culture change. Look up your own name, or start with a comparison.

        What sociologists see in names

        Fashion without a seller. Sociologist Stanley Lieberson studied names because nobody profits from them. When a name spreads, it’s spreading through people’s tastes and their sense of what other parents are choosing.

        Parents want a name that’s familiar but not too common. That’s why popular names burn out. Once a name is everywhere, it starts to sound like the last generation, and parents move on. Try Linda, Jennifer, and Jason.

        Names can change gender, usually in one direction. Ashley and Leslie were boys’ names first. Once many girls had them, parents mostly stopped giving them to boys. Try the “From boys to girls” comparison.

        Old names come back. Names tied to grandparents sound dated; names tied to great-grandparents can sound fresh. Emma, Hazel, Theodore, and Oliver all fell for decades before returning.

        Pop culture matters less than you’d think. Movies and shows can launch a name (Madison first appeared on the list in 1985, the year after the movie Splash), but Lieberson found that many names credited to a celebrity were already rising before the celebrity was famous.

        Names carry signals about class, race, and generation. In a well-known experiment, economists sent out identical résumés with different names; those with names more common among White Americans got more callbacks than those with names more common among Black Americans.

        Talk about it

        1. Look up your own name and a parent’s or grandparent’s. What do the lines tell you about when each of you was born?
        2. Girls’ names have changed faster than boys’ names for most of the past century. Why might parents feel freer to be creative with girls’ names?
        3. Why do you think names move from boys to girls much more often than from girls to boys?
        4. What does someone’s first name lead you to assume about their age, background, or family? Where did you learn those associations?
        5. If nobody is selling names, what makes so many parents choose the same ones in the same years?

        About the data

        Ranks are the Social Security Administration’s lists of the 1,000 most popular names for girls and for boys each year from 1880 to 2025, based on Social Security card applications for U.S. births. Different spellings count as different names (Sophia and Sofia). Many people born before 1937 never applied for a card, so the early years are less complete. The lists record sex as female or male, as it appears on the application.

        Sources: Social Security Administration, “Popular Baby Names”, compiled from Arul John’s copy of the SSA lists. Lieberson, Stanley. 2000. A Matter of Taste: How Names, Fashions, and Culture Change. Yale University Press. Bertrand, Marianne and Sendhil Mullainathan. 2004. “Are Emily and Greg More Employable than Lakisha and Jamal?” American Economic Review 94(4): 991–1013.
